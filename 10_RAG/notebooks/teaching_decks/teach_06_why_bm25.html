Why BM25, and not just TF-IDF?

We already know TF-IDF. Today we find the one thing wrong with it, and the one idea that fixes it. That idea is BM25.

Before we start, the obvious question

We already have dense embeddings. Why learn a second retriever?

Last class we saw embeddings place meaning into a few hundred dimensions, so "how do plants make food using light" lands right next to "photosynthesis converts sunlight into glucose" even though the two sentences share almost no words. That part is real and it works.

What embeddings are genuinely good at: paraphrase, synonyms, intent, meaning that survives when the wording changes completely.

So here is the fair question to ask before we spend a whole notebook on something older than embeddings: if meaning already works, what is left to fix?

1
No guarantee on exact tokens
An error code, a SKU, a citation gets folded into a vector like every other word. Embeddings compare "roughly similar," they were never built to promise "this exact string is present."
2
The wins are often razor thin
In our own notebook 4 numbers, dense embeddings found the right document on a tricky query, but its top score beat the second place score by as little as 0.06. Correct today, one similar document away from wrong.
3
Every query costs a model call
Dense search needs an embedding model to run at query time. That is latency and, on a hosted API, money. Per query, every time.
4
You cannot ask it why
A cosine similarity of 0.548 is just a number. It does not tell you which words mattered or how rare they were. Nothing to debug when it ranks something oddly.
Dense embeddings are not wrong, they are fuzzy by design. That fuzziness is exactly what makes them bad at exact, rare, cheap, explainable matching. BM25 is the tool built for precisely that gap, which is why production search almost always runs both.

The problem

Raw counts have no ceiling

Two documents, both about dogs. Same query: "dog".

1×
Doc 1 says "dog" once
You read it. It's about a dog.
100×
Doc 2 says "dog" a hundred times
You already knew that by the fifth mention.
TF-IDF's answer: Doc 2 is exactly 100 times more relevant than Doc 1. Double the count, double the score, forever, with no limit.

Is that actually true?

What BM25 changes

Two curves, one argument

Same chart, both formulas

TF-IDF keeps climbing forever. BM25 rises fast at first, then flattens and hits a wall.

Zoomed in: just the BM25 shape

The first few mentions matter the most. Mention six, ten and fifty barely move the needle.

TF-IDF trusts the raw count and rewards repetition forever. BM25 says the first few mentions prove the topic, and every mention after that matters less and less. That is called saturation, and it is the whole idea.

The same argument, in numbers

By the time you've repeated a word 20 times, BM25 has basically made up its mind. TF-IDF is still climbing, and will keep climbing however long you keep typing the word.

Under the hood

What actually changed in the formula

unchanged
IDF, rarity
Same idea as TF-IDF. A word that appears in 1 of 16 documents is worth far more than a word in all 16.
new
TF, but capped
Controlled by a knob called k1. It sets how quickly repetition stops paying off. Typical value: 1.2 to 2.0.
new
Length normalization
Controlled by a knob called b. A long document doesn't win just for having more words to repeat things in. Typical value: 0.75.
as word count → infinity, BM25's term score → (k1 + 1), never further

Back to our own corpus

This is why BM25 won decisively today

doc 0: "Error ERR_CONN_TIMEOUT occurs when the database connection exceeds the 30 second limit..."
query: "ERR_CONN_TIMEOUT"
BM25 top1, score 0.826 Dense top1, score 0.548, but fuzzy

ERR_CONN_TIMEOUT appears in exactly one of our sixteen documents. That gives it a huge IDF. It only needs to appear once for BM25 to be certain, so the saturation cap never even gets tested. Rare, exact tokens are BM25's home turf, and its score tells you exactly why it's confident. Dense also finds it here, but as a softer, harder to explain signal.

Being honest about the limit

Saturation doesn't fix everything

doc 4: "Myocardial infarction results from coronary artery occlusion, causing ischemic damage to cardiac muscle."
query: "what is a heart attack?"
BM25 score, 0.000

Not one word is shared between the query and the document. BM25 only ever looks at literal tokens, so a perfect saturation formula still scores this a flat zero. Fixing raw counts was never going to fix vocabulary gaps, that is exactly where dense embeddings take over, and neither method alone covers both jobs.

A third option

Meet SPLADE: sparse, but learned

BM25 counts words that are literally there. Dense turns a whole sentence into one blurry vector. SPLADE sits in between: it still builds a sparse vector, one slot per vocabulary word, exactly like BM25's index. The difference is who decides the weight in each slot.

On this small, clean corpus, dense already hits everything, so SPLADE has nothing left to prove on hit rate alone. Its real edge is underneath the hit or miss: it's a sparse, inverted-index-friendly vector like BM25, so it plugs into keyword-style search infrastructure, and it stays inspectable, you can list the exact weighted words behind a score, which a dense vector never gives you. The cost is real too: a full neural network runs per document and per query, closer to dense's cost than to BM25's instant lookup.

Why this isn't just academic

Every major search engine made this switch

1970s–90s
TF-IDF, the original ranking idea
2015–16
Lucene, then Elasticsearch, switch their default to BM25
Today
BM25 is still the standard sparse retriever, paired with dense search

This is not a minor tuning detail. It is the reason keyword search on any real product, from your codebase search to your email search, behaves the way it does.
